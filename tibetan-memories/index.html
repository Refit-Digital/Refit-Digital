The cook

Namdi learnt this food at home, and in a monastery

He was born in Riwoche, in Kham, in 1976. Life was hard, and he and his brothers and sisters all worked from a young age. There are about twelve of them, he thinks, though he can't say exactly. He left the Tibetan plateau at twenty.

He made his way to Belgium by way of Nepal and India, and spent years working split shifts, six days a week, in a Michelin recommended Italian restaurant in Antwerp. He never learnt Flemish or French. He does know a good few insults in Sardinian.

Some Tibetan staples don't travel well. Tsampa, roasted barley, is a hard sell to anyone who didn't grow up on it. So he kept what works and built the rest around fresh green vegetables, which the plateau never had much of and the West has in abundance. Produce often comes from family and friends' allotments.

A craft and a meditation.

That's how he describes making torma, the coloured offerings built from edible ingredients that he learnt in his monastery. The same attention goes into a tray of momos. He sometimes brings torma to the stall to show people. They're never for sale.

The food is vegan because of what he was taught there, not because of a trend. In his words, the family would rather not profit from others' suffering.

This site sets no cookies

There is no cookie banner here because there is nothing to consent to. Nothing is stored on your device and nothing is read from it: no cookies, no local storage, no tracking pixels, no advertising tags. That is unusual, and it is deliberate.

Because nothing is stored on or read from your device, the consent requirement in regulation 6 of the Privacy and Electronic Communications (EC Directive) Regulations 2003 does not apply to this site.

What is collected, and why

  • Visits to this page. We use Google Analytics to count visits and see which parts of the page people read. It is set up not to store anything on your device and to shorten your IP address before it is recorded, so we cannot identify you and cannot follow you from one visit to the next. The lawful basis is our legitimate interest in knowing whether the site is doing its job.
  • The calendar of dates. The dates on this page come from the family's public Google Calendar. Loading it means Google receives your IP address and basic details about your browser.
  • The typeface. The site's typeface is served by Google Fonts, with the same consequence: Google receives your IP address and browser details when the font loads.
  • Hosting. The site is hosted on GitHub Pages, and like any web host GitHub's servers see the IP address of everyone who asks for a page.
  • Emails and phone calls. If you write or ring about a booking we keep what you send: your name, how to reach you, the date, the place, and whatever you tell us about your event. We use it to answer you and to run the booking. The lawful basis is taking steps at your request before entering into a contract, and afterwards our legitimate interest in keeping a record of work we have done.

What we do not do

There is no contact form, no account to create, no payment taken on this site, no newsletter, no advertising and no profiling. We do not build a picture of you, we do not make automated decisions about you, and we do not sell or share anyone's details for anyone else's purposes. We do not send marketing emails or texts; if that ever changed we would ask you first, as the Privacy and Electronic Communications (EC Directive) Regulations 2003 require.

Who else sees it

Google, for the analytics, the calendar, the typeface and our email, which is a Gmail account; and GitHub, for the hosting. Nobody else, unless we are required by law to hand something over.

Where it goes

Google and GitHub are United States companies, so your information may be handled outside the United Kingdom. Both state that they use the safeguards UK data protection law requires for those transfers.

How long it is kept

Analytics reports are counts, not records about individuals. Enquiry emails stay in the Gmail account [for how long — to be decided by Namdi and Elissa. A stated period, for instance three years after the event, is worth more than "as long as we need it".]
